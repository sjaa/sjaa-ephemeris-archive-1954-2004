# The Passing of an SJAA Legend
Credit: Rob Jaworski

On the eve of the American annular eclipse of October 2023, the members of SJAA were provided with some sad news. We learned that long time SJAA member Jim Van Nuland passed away earlier in the month.

Jim joined up with the SJAA in the mid-1970s, maybe earlier. He served many roles on the board and in officer positions, but most notably he was the incredible energy of the School Star Party program. Indeed, for decades Jim *was* the School Star Party program. During the school year Jim and his crew were out on some elementary, middle school or high school campus showing the wonders of the night sky to every next generation. He would schedule so many SSPs, sometimes three a week, sometimes seven. It kept him busy and he loved it. His only breaks were around the full moon when he was able to convince eager parents and administrators that they really don't want to look at a 98% lit moon through a telescope. Sometimes he couldn't convince them and so out he went. Jim kept at it until he was physically unable to carry it on any longer and finally retired in 2018.

The family posted this obituary online, but we have pasted it below as well. https://danerimortuary.com/tribute/details/324140/James-Van-Nuland/obituary.html

*James "Jim" Henry Van Nuland, 87, of Little Chute, and Tigerton Wisconsin, passed away on September 30, 2023, in Folsom, California. Born on September 14, 1936, Jim was the eldest son of the late Jacob and Verna Van Nuland.*

*Jim had a fulfilling life and left behind a loving family. He is survived by his sons Patrick, Raymond, Thomas, and Michael, and his "Favorite Daughter," Barbara. His sisters Joan and Rosemary are also blessedly still with us.*

*Jim was gifted with 16 grandchildren, 12 great-grandchildren, and countless fans and friends who gravitated toward his kindness, and some would say, innocence. He was preceded in death by his beloved wife of 44 years, Florence-Anna, who passed away in 2004.*

*After completing high school in 1954, Jim proudly served in the US Navy for 3 years, completing 2 scientific tours to Antarctica, and mapping the ocean floor east of Florida, over which all of NASA's rockets would eventually take off.*

*Following his military service, Jim enrolled at Marquette University to study Electrical Engineering but was in the end, "drafted" by IBM to become a Programmer where he dedicated 25 years of his life, earning a 30-year credited early retirement. His expertise and hard work made a significant impact on the company, even to this day.*

*He was one of the first "civilians" with a personal computer during the '70s, and instilled the "tech-bug" in his kids as early as he could.*

*It was early in college when he double-dated with his sister, and her date and met Florence, -whom he was then forever enamored with, married, and had 5 children. 2 born in Wisconsin, 2 in Southern California, and saving the "best" one, born last in San Jose California, where he lived since 1966.*

*In his free time, Jim pursued his passion for astronomy and spent many hours sharing his knowledge as a 40-year member of the San Jose Amateur Astronomy Association, with over an estimated 100,000 children over the decades. He was a curious man who found joy in exploring the wonders of the universe and sharing it with everyone. He claimed he was "Paid with wows" when asked about any compensation for his tireless efforts. He was paid well. May he rest in peace, and his skies be always clear.*

*Jim's faith played an important role in his life, sending his children to Catholic schools, and was a long-time member of Saint Lucy's Catholic Church.*

*Services for Jim Van Nuland will be announced at a later date. His legacy will live on through his family, friends, and all those whose lives he touched.*

Jay Reynolds Freeman, upon hearing about Jim's demise, wrote the following.

*I remember Jim well -- he and his eight-inch equatorially-mounted Newtonian were regular fixtures at SJAA events during the several decades in which I was an active local member.*

*Astronomy folks may be less familiar with his experiences in computer science, which he occasionally mentioned while waiting for sunset or for transient clouds to pass. I remember a time in the late 1990s, when Jim told of the scorn with which IBM management greeted his mid-1960s suggestion that it would be unwise to use only two digits for years in text strings representing dates, because some day the calendar would roll through a century year, the digits would wrap around, and confusion might arise thereby. It did, and the "Y2K" problem was the result. Jim also convinced computer operators that yes, it was indeed possible for a programmer to cause serious physical damage to a mainframe computer, supposedly safe in its isolated room, merely by running code: His program used the track-seek function on a large, heavy, early disk drive to slam the read-write head between its radial limits at a varying rate. When that rate matched the resonant frequency for lateral oscillations of the entire drive unit, cabinet and all, that unit started walking across the floor of the computer room and eventually tipped over.*

*Rest in peace, Jim ...*

*-- Jay Reynolds Freeman*

The family thought it would be appropriate to hold a celebration of Jim's life at Houge Park, the SJAA basecamp for so many years (and club lore has it that Jim had a hand in securing the park for the club sometime in the 1990s). Of course it was most appropriate, and several current club members helped to host the event there, which was very well attended. Marianne Damon, the SJAA refreshments lead, was able to attend both the funeral and the life celebration. These are her words.

*The funeral mass was held on Monday, October 23 at his parish church, St. Lucy's. Several SJAA members attended in support of the family. The urn chosen was of a nebular design and made us smile, knowing he would have approved of the choice. The organist who played did so lovingly, as Jim would always go up and thank her and the choir after each mass.*

*The family asked to have the post-funeral gathering at Houge Park, and SJAA was able to host this event. We opened the hall and set up tables with the family before the funeral. They had requested that, if possible, they be able to see the two telescopes they had donated, and we were able to oblige. Two of Jim's sons brought them out from behind the curtain and set them up. JVN's daughter Barbara recalled how the big white 8" dob got its name: "When the big 8" stood in the backyard all those years, wrapped up in layers protected from the elements, it reminded Mom of Ruth Buzzi's bag lady character from the Laugh-In TV show: Gladys." After the funeral, family and friends gathered at Houge Hall and reminisced about our adventures with Jim. The family had set up a memorial table to which we added the trophy given by the City of San Jose for service to the community, mostly for his years of providing school star parties. The family provided Jim's favorite snacks and lunch items. We concluded the gathering with thoughts of a fond farewell to our astronomy friend and mentor, JVN.*

As the SJAA's sole two-time recipient of the Gregory Award (1984 and 2007), the club's highest honor, Jim was held in high regard and he will continue to be. With his passing the club turns a chapter in its history, moving from an organization started in the mid-twentieth century with the awe inspired by the launch of Sputnik and the myriad NASA programs, to the twenty-first century where professional space science (and computer science!) continues. The SJAA will carry on as Jim would want, to provide a space for like minded individuals to gather as well as to provide a place where the next generation of enthusiasts (and hopefully scientists and engineers) can find a foothold.

Thank you for everything you did, Jim.

# Scientific Process At Work and The Formation Of Our Solar System

Credit: Jordan Makower

![Solar System Illustration](Image from www.tweaktown.com)

*Image from www.tweaktown.com*

Nicolaus Copernicus, in 1543, proposed the idea that planets orbited the Sun, in seeking to find a simple model to account for his observations and the re-evaluation existing data about the irregular movements of planets. A few decades after that, Tycho Brahe collected much greater amounts of measurements on planetary motions which supported the Copernican Theory. That data eventually fell into the hands of Johannes Kepler, who formulated his 3 Laws of Planetary Motion (Elliptic orbits, Equal Areas Swept by Planets in Equal Time, Relationship between a Planet's revolution and its distance from the Sun). Each of these people had never used a telescope. When Galileo adapted telescopic observations to his predecessors' work, the issue of planetary movements was *scientifically settled*.

Over 100 years later, Immanuel Kant, building on work done by other scientists, proposed a Nebular Hypothesis, in which gaseous clouds move and gravitationally collapse to form stars and planets. In 1796, Pierre-Simon Laplace proposed a more detailed model considering the formation of our Sun and planets.

This is how 'scientific process' works; observations lead to unifying ideas (theories), which are tested and accepted or rejected, based on data collected. Theories are revised as new information is obtained, and our understanding of our environment is improved, even if not completely explained.

In order to explain the features we observe in our Solar System; size/composition of Sun and planets, planetary rings, moons, asteroids, magnetic/energy fields, comets…, we apply all the data we have gathered to understand its process of formation.

Our Solar System has formed, and is forming, from the gravitational compaction of matter generated by the last cloud of debris from previous nearby supernovas, which produced the 118 chemical elements existing in our 'neighborhood' today. NASA and other scientific organizations around the world continue to collect data to aid in a more complete picture of our surroundings. Be alert for new discoveries to improve our 'model'.

# On the Road: Annular Eclipse Viewing From Battle Mountain Nevada

Credit: Joe Fragola

By now, the excitement over the annular Solar eclipse on October 14 has subsided. As the date for the eclipse approached astronomy enthusiasts, and the public in general, made plans on where to best view the eclipse. Given that the date for the eclipse fell on a Saturday, local eclipse watchers had some flexibility in deciding whether to remain at home in the Bay Area to view a partial eclipse, or travel for an opportunity to view totality. The SJAA Observers Google group captured posts about the SJAA-sponsored viewing at Houge Park, SJAA support of an eclipse viewing party in Palo Alto organized by Stanford University, as well as separate plans being made by SJAA Members.

Those traveling to view the eclipse had concerns about two key factors – lodging and weather. As it turned out a small contingent from SJAA gathered in the small community of Battle Mountain, Nevada. This location is a bit over 200 miles east of Reno, about a 3-hour drive by car. The town was hosting an eclipse viewing party in Elquist Park, a nice grassy area located between their Recreation Center and the High School. At one point, the size of the SJAA group planning to watch from Battle Mountain was 9 Members. Due to concerns over the weather forecast on Eclipse Day, a few Members made alternate plans. In the end, SJAA representation totaled 5 Members (plus two family members).

![Group photo of six people standing on grass with chairs and equipment, trees and mountains in background](image)

*SJAA group in Battle Mountain (left to right: Marianne's sister Jan, Karen Bieber, Marianne Damon, Vignesh Balaji, Milind Girkar, Joe Fragola. Not pictured: Debbie Fragola who took the picture.)*

On the morning of the eclipse, we woke up to completely cloudy skies. The SJAA group gathered in the park by 7:30 a.m. Prospects for viewing didn't look good. There were breaks in the clouds and some patches of blue sky toward the south/southeast. The clouds were moving slowly, in the right direction to give us an opportunity to eventually see an unobscured Sun. But it still wasn't certain whether we would get an opportunity to view totality or not. The race was between slowly moving clouds, and orbital mechanics bringing the Sun-Moon-Earth alignment closer.

---

From our location, the eclipse began at 8:08 a.m. Our first break came around 8:30 a.m. when we were able to view the Sun in a partial eclipse. Partly cloudy sky conditions continued for the next 50 minutes with the cloud cover seeming to thin out a bit as time went on. Luckily, we had enough of a break to view the 4 minutes and 18 seconds of totality between 9:20 am and 9:24 a.m. After that, we were back to intermittent clouds for a while as we watched the partial phase as the Moon finished crossing the Sun.

Marianne was the most prepared among us, having several pair of binoculars with solar filters which she shared with the group. The best views were through her tripod-mounted 16 x 70 binoscope. In addition to those of us who did not have observing equipment, many of the other eclipse watchers were very appreciative of the views Marianne shared through her binoscope. Sometimes a little patience was required as the Sun/Moon played hide and seek behind a passing cloud. The local tourism bureau had planned for eclipse shades to be given out "one per family". Upon learning of this, Karen and Marianne brought lots of extras so everyone could have a pair. Luckily, when the clouds parted for our first view, everyone was able to look at the same time. Everyone had a chance at a great view, including several sunspot groups during the partial phases of the eclipse through Marianne's setup.

![Eclipse photo](image)

*(Photo by Vignesh Balaji using a Galaxy smartphone with a solar filter covering the lenses.)*

With the October eclipse behind us, the cosmic clock continues to tick. If you missed the annular Solar eclipse, you still have the opportunity to experience a total Solar eclipse on April 8, 2024. Here's a link to help with planning for this eclipse. https://nationaleclipse.com/maps.html

Once again, the Bay Area will be outside the path of totality, so a road trip will be necessary for the next eclipse. At the time of writing this article (late October), it's already getting late to find lodging for this eclipse. As you are reading this in the December issue of The Ephemeris, reservations are even more scarce with the event only 4 months away.

Happy eclipse viewing everyone and good luck with the weather and lodging wherever you choose to watch.

# Oct 14th Partial Eclipse Event in Houge Park

Credit: Chris Gottbrath

On Oct 14th we hosted a very successful public viewing of the partial eclipse that was visible from San Jose. We had about 300 people join us at Houge Park to see the eclipse through eclipse glasses as well as through the many telescopes we made available.

![Image showing a diverse crowd at Houge Park during the eclipse event](Image by: Christos Gougoussis. A diverse crowd takes in eclipse at Houge Park)

*Image by: Christos Gougoussis. A diverse crowd takes in eclipse at Houge Park*

*(Attendee faces have been blurred here and in other images to preserve privacy.)*

Weather was a challenge, indeed the first part of the eclipse at around 8:10 was fairly obscured by clouds. However there were breaks which, I suppose, added a bit to the drama. I was delighted to hear the crowd react each time the clouds parted and some folks could see the eclipse for the first time while others got a chance to see how much more of the sun was obscured by the moon.

As the morning progressed, the cloud cover broke up a bit and from mid-eclipse at 9:20 on we had a pretty consistent view with only a few lengthy interruptions.

While the eclipse itself was an annular eclipse, the path of annularity passed north and east of San Jose (touching just the far north east corner of California) so we got to see a pretty respectable partial eclipse. We set up the event to allow participants to see the eclipse as it was happening for us locally and also be able to watch live streams of the view from other locations along the path of annularity.

I want to make sure this article thanks all the folks who volunteered to make the event a success. I need to start with Radhika Gupta who stepped forward and helped out across the whole event. Planning and executing the morning's activity took thought and effort and it was fantastic to be able to count on a strong co-leader.

Peter Melhus and Snehal Shinde had food, drink, and solar beads available for those interested. On their way back outside to take in the eclipse with their glasses, participants had the opportunity to see and interact with a scale model demonstration of the eclipse that Gary Mitchell organized.

Feedback from folks outside included that some of the observing locations were obscured by a tree. We'd noted it as a concern but didn't carve out quite enough of the space when we created the planning map for folks setting up their scopes. Another bit of feedback was that we needed more ways (stools, ladders) to help kids get in good positions to look at the eyepieces of various scopes.

Glenn Newell has a custom Wi-Fi based image streaming solution that he has been developing. He set that up and with a couple of QR codes attendees could use their phones to attach to his Wi-Fi and then point their browsers to his stream. This technique supports a relatively large number of folks being able to see what his telescope is observing at one time. This has potential application to public observing sessions and contrasts with what you get from some of the EAA solutions that typically only allow a single device to connect at a time.

I used a portable Wi-Fi hotspot (a Netgear Nighthawk M6 on AT&T) to provide Wi-Fi coverage out near the telescopes. Nikola Nikolov and I connected our computers to that Wi-Fi which gave a stronger signal than the SJAA one in the building. We used the SJAA Zoom license to create a meeting and Nikola shared his telescope view in the Zoom meeting. I set up a live YouTube stream for my images. With both sources available Rich and the folks inside could choose either of our images to display on the screen. Working out the streaming and the projector setup took some practice. I got great advice and help on networking and streaming tech from Parag Shah during our prep sessions – Thanks Parag!

One thing that I learned the hard way during the event was that my manual equatorial mount, while fine for tracking the sun during a normal observing run without clouds, didn't perform well during the eclipse. With the intermittent clouds I lost track of the sun multiple times and then getting it back involved switching back to a low power eyepiece and then having to refocus for the camera. That meant my livestream had gaps when I wasn't showing the eclipse. Luckily Nikola's tracking mount was better able to follow the sun even when it was behind a cloud. We don't have a recording of the Zoom but edited a version of my live stream to skip or speed through the time spent re-acquiring the sun which you can look up by searching YouTube for "Chris Gottbrath Eclipse Edited".

## A Name Change
Credit: Marianne Damon

The International Dark-Sky Association (IDA) has changed its name to DarkSky International. So instead of IDA, think DarkSky!

Awareness of the importance of dark skies continues to grow, and DarkSky is the main driver of educating people at the local, regional and international levels. In the United States they partner with the National Park Service, Audubon, National Science Foundation, United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs, the Illuminating Engineering Society (IES), and the International Commission of Illumination. How effective have they been? A listing of their 2023 accomplishments mention certifying ten new International Dark Sky Places, including the first in mainland China; enrolling new DarkSky chapters in Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay, New Mexico, and Kenya (the first on the African continent); reaching one billion people (!) during International Dark Sky Week; and hosting Under One Sky, a global 24-hour virtual conference.

Of interest, DarkSky hosts an online monthly presentation called Night Matters. The speaker for the recent November 15th event titled Festivals of Light was Michael Marlin, a long-time advocate for dark sky education. He has just written the first book on the growing interest in "star tourism", Astrotourism: Star Gazers, Eclipse Chasers, and the Dark Sky Movement. He is also working to produce a one-hour weekly television series, The Astrotourist, "which takes a deep cultural dive into humanity's relationship with the stars and how light pollution is impacting the biosphere across the planet."

# SJAA Library

![SJAA Library](image of bookshelves)

SJAA offers another wonderful resource; a library with good astronomy books and DVDs available to all of our members that will interest all age groups and especially young children who are budding astronomers! You may still send all your questions/comments to librarian@sjaa.net.

## Telescope Fix It Session

Fix It Day, sometimes called the Telescope Tune Up or the Telescope Fix It program is a real simple service the SJAA offers to members of the community for free, though it's priceless. Headed up by Larry Zurbrick, the Fix It session provides a place for people to come with their telescope or other astronomy gear problems and have them looked at, such as broken scopes whose owners need advice, or need help with collimating a telescope.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/fixit/

## Solar Observing

Solar observing sessions, headed up by Wolf Witt, are usually held the 1st Sunday of every Month from 2pm - 4pm at Houge Park weather permitting. Please check SJ Astronomy Meetup for schedule details as the event time / location is subject to change.

http://www.meetup.com/SJ-Astronomy/

## Quick STARt Program

The Quick STARt Program, headed up by Jonathan Lawton , helps to ease folks into amateur astronomy. You have to admit, astronomy can look exciting from the outside, but once you scratch the surface, it can get seemingly complex in a hurry. But it doesn't have to be that way if there's someone to guide you and answer all your seemingly basic questions.

The Quick Start sessions are generally held quarterly. The Quick Start program is currently on hold pending a leader stepping up to own the program.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/quick-start/

## Intro to the Night Sky

The Intro to the Night Sky session takes place monthly, in conjunction with first quarter moon and In Town Star Parties at Houge Park. This is a regular, monthly session, run by David Grover, each with a similar format, with only the content changing to reflect what's currently in the night sky. After the session, the attendees will go outside for a guided, green laser tour of the sky, along with views through SJAA Telescopes at the In-Town Star Party, to get a better look at the night's celestial objects.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/beginners-astronomy/

## Loaner Program

Muditha Kanchana (Kanch) heads up this program. The Program goal is for SJAA members to be able to evaluate equipment they are considering purchasing or are just curious about by checking out loaners from SJAA's growing list of equipment. Please note that certain items have restrictions or special conditions that must be met.

If you are an SJAA member and an experienced observer or have been through the SJAA Quick STARt program please fill this form to request a particular item. Please also consider donating unused equipment.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/loaner-telescope-program/

## Astro Imaging Special Interest Group (SIG)

SIG has a mission of bringing together people who have an interest in astronomy imaging, or put more simply, taking pictures of the night sky. Run by Hy Murveit, the Imaging SIG meets roughly every month at Houge Park to discuss topics about imaging. The SIG is open to people with absolutely no experience but want to learn what it's all about, but experienced imagers are also more than welcome, indeed, encouraged to participate. The best way to get involved is to review the postings on the SJAA Astro Imaging mail list in Google Groups.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/imaging-sig/

## Astro Imaging Workshops and Field Clinics

Not to be confused with the SIG group this newly organized program championed by Glenn Newell is a hands on program for club members, who are interested in astrophotography, to have a chance of seeing what it is all about.

Workshops are held at Houge Park once per month and field clinics (members only) once per quarter at a dark sky site. Check the schedule and contact Glenn Newell if you are interested.

## School Star Party

The San Jose Astronomical Association conducts evening observing sessions (commonly called "star parties") for schools in mid-Santa Clara County, generally from Sunnyvale to Fremont to Morgan Hill.

Contact SJAA's (Program Coordinator) for additional information.

http://www.sjaa.net/programs/school-star-party/
