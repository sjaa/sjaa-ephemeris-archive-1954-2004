# SJAA Member Programs

## SJAA LIBRARY

SJAA offers another wonderful resource; a library with good astronomy books and DVDs available to all of our members that will interest all age groups and especially young children who are budding astronomers! You may still send all your questions/comments to **librarian@sjaa.net**.

## TELESCOPE FIX-IT SESSION

Fix-It Day, sometimes called the Telescope Tune Up or the Telescope Fix-It program is a really simple service the SJAA offers to members of the community for free, though it's priceless. Headed up by Larry Zurbrick, the Fix It session provides a place for people to come with their telescope or other astronomy gear problems and have them looked at, such as broken scopes whose owners need advice, or need help with collimating a telescope.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/fixit/**

## SOLAR OBSERVING

Solar observing sessions, headed up by Wolf Witt, are usually held the 1st Sunday of every Month from 2pm - 4pm at Houge Park weather permitting. Please check SJ Astronomy Meetup for schedule details as the event time and location is subject to change.

**http://www.meetup.com/SJ-Astronomy/**

## QUICK START PROGRAM

The Quick STARt Program, headed up by Jonathan Lawton , helps to ease folks into amateur astronomy. You have to admit, astronomy can look exciting from the out- side, but once you scratch the surface, it can get seemingly complex in a hurry. But it doesn't have to be that way if there's someone to guide you and answer all your seemingly basic questions.

The Quick STARt sessions are generally held quarterly. The Quick STARt program has resumed under its new lead, Jonathon Lawton.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/quick-start/**

## ASTRONOMY 101

Astronomy 101 covers various topics related to beginner astronomy and star gazing. The popular Intro to the Night Sky session takes place monthly, in conjunction with first quarter moon and In Town Star Parties at Hogue Park. This is a regular, monthly session, run by Carl Svensson. Content varies from "what's up in the night sky" to basics of star-gazing and telescopes to specific topics of interest. After the session, the attendees will go outside for a guided, green laser tour of the sky, along with views through SJAA Telescopes at the In-Town Star Party, to get a better look at the night's celestial objects.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/beginners-astronomy/**

## LOANER PROGRAM

Muditha Kanchana (Kanch) heads up this program. The Program goal is for SJAA members to be able to evaluate equipment they are considering purchasing or are just curious about by checking out loaners from SJAA's growing list of equipment. Please note that certain items have restrictions or special conditions that must be met.

If you are an SJAA member and an experienced observer or have been through the SJAA Quick STARt program please fill this form to request a particular item. Please also consider donating unused equipment.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/loaner-telescope-program/**

## SCHOOL STAR PARTY

The San Jose Astronomical Association conducts evening observing sessions (commonly called "star parties") for schools in mid-Santa Clara County, generally from Sunnyvale to Fremont to Morgan Hill. Contact SJAA's Program Coordinator for additional information.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/school-star-party/**

# ASTRO IMAGING SPECIAL INTEREST GROUP

SIG has a mission of bringing together people who have an interest in astronomy imaging, or put more simply, taking pictures of the night sky. Run by Hy Murveit, the Imaging SIG meets roughly every month at Hogue Park to discuss topics about imaging. The SIG is open to people with absolutely no experience but want to learn what it's all about, but experienced imagers are also more than welcome, indeed, encouraged to participate. The best way to get involved is to review the postings on the SJAA Astro Imaging mail list in Google Groups.

**http://www.sjaa.net/programs/imaging-sig/**

## ASTRO IMAGING WORKSHOPS AND FIELD CLINICS

Not to be confused with the Imaging SIG group this newly organized program championed by Glenn Newell is a hands on program for club members, who are interested in astro- photography, to have a chance of seeing what it is all about.

Workshops are held at Hogue Park once per month and field clinics (members only) once per quarter at a dark sky site. Check the schedule and contact Glenn Newell if you are interested.

# Happy 70th Anniversary, SJAA!

**By Marianne Damon and Rob Jaworski**

This year, 2024, marks the 70th anniversary of SJAA's founding. Members, family, and friends of San Jose Astronomical Association are invited to share in the festivities marking our 70th year! We will hold a potluck party, with beverages and dessert provided. There might even be some games and prizes! Meanwhile, please enjoy these bits of history, as documented on our webpage, and a first-hand account of SJAA throughout the decades from long-time member, Bob Fingerhut.

## SOME HISTORY FROM OUR WEBSITE

In the Nov. 8 1954 issue of the San Jose Evening News, columnist Dick Barrett ran a small article asking "Any Amateur Astronomers?" at the behest of William Weller. The item brought a positive response.

Weller called the first meeting at Herbert Hoover Jr. High in San Jose on December 6, 1954. Twenty people were present. A member of the Peninsula Astronomical Society spoke on astronomy clubs, then gave a discussion of the two main types of astronomical telescopes, listing the advantages and disadvantages of each. Next, Dr. Eggen of Lick Observatory spoke on "The Star of Bethlehem", listing various possible astronomical explanations.

The meeting was a success, and the time and place for the next meeting were decided. By May, the first Board of Directors and officers were elected, and by August 26, Articles of Incorporation were written and submitted to Sacramento, bringing the club into legal existence.

Jump to October 1978, when the name was changed from San Jose Amateur Astronomers to San Jose Astronomical Association, at the suggestion of past president A. Boris Gregory. His concern was that the word "amateur" is often interpreted in a negative way, as in "mere amateur" or "amateurish".

Over the years, the SJAA has met at schools, city parks, colleges, libraries, as conditions permitted. The move to Houge Park is the newest of those, in May 1995; though Houge had been used for public star parties from April 1992.

## SOME HISTORY FROM THE EPHEMERIS

Many years ago, the calendar on the first page of our newsletter was marked "Ephemeris" to mean a table of coming events. One day we received a request from the librarian at NASA/AMES, asking for certain month's issues of the "SJAA Ephemeris". That's how it came to be named that; before, it had no actual title.

The earliest issue of the Ephemeris on our website is

January 1999, Volume 10, No. 1. We have no records of the beginning Volumes 1-9, and we don't know when the paper version transitioned into an online presence. In that January issue Ed Erbeck is listed as president. Akkhana Peck wrote her "The Shallow Sky" feature, and the recently deceased, world-famous comet hunter Don Machholz, wrote his regular feature, "Comet Comments." Dave North, stepping down after two years as editor, writes: "It's been an amazing couple of years, an experience I'll never forget. You get to meet and work with some great people doing this job, particularly Don Machholtz and Richard Stanton (with their regular columns) and other retired editors (such as the tireless John Gleason). But enough about the old. It's time to ring in the new year, and the new editors, from whom we can all expect nothing but the best. This is, of course, my last opportunity to stand on the soap box, a practice I think I've generally avoided. But hey, one last shot. When I started this job, the club was in something of a crisis, and several board members resigned. Since that time, things have stabilized somewhat, and we've helped rebuild the observatory at Fremont Peak, a contribution we can all take pride in. So things are okay, but this could be a much better club. It was built by people who are now legends in the amateur community, and we have quite a history to live up to. Perhaps we can. Overall, it's an exciting time, and I hope to see more folks getting involved and taking up the banner."

## SOME HISTORY FROM BOB FINGERHUT

*When I joined the club we were meeting at Olinder Center in San Jose. It was a great place and I wish we had stayed there. Someone knew someone at the Rosicrucian Museum and Planetarium (The Egyptian Museum) and moved us to the planetarium. We were there about 2 years, the membership increased, the planetarium was ok as a lecture hall, but it did not have good parking and was not available on non-meeting nights. We did not have use of the projector and it was always left in position for the museum's next show. I do not know what happened, but someone must have touched something and we were kicked out with no notice.*

*That began the hunt for a meeting place. We met in a classroom at Santa Clara University for a while. Sometimes no one showed up to let us in. Ed Schell found a school for a few meetings and then Ed got us into the Red Cross Building in Los Gatos. We were there for many years and it was a great place. We had access every Saturday night and it had good parking. The club prospered there. We started the club auction so that we could contribute to the Red Cross and keep the price of operating the club and printing the newsletter down. Unfortunately, the Red Cross closed the building and we needed to find a new place. Someone found Houge*

# splashdown 55

By Carl Svensson

*Guests enjoyed great views through all telescopes, EAA scopes, and binoculars hosted by SJAA volunteers atop the USS Hornet Flight Deck on July 20th, 2024. Scopes on hand included white light and hydrogen-alpha solar scopes. Image credit: Rob Jaworski*

The Apollo 11 command module made its famous "Splashdown" landing in the Pacific Ocean, near Hawaii on July 24th, 1969. Fifty-five years later, we celebrate this historic event onboard the USS Hornet; the now-decommissioned aircraft carrier that recovered the Apollo 11 crew. SJAA was on-hand to show off views of the sun, and regale guests with fun facts and tales about our own personal star.

But first, we had to get ourselves and all of our gear up to the flight deck! We were given the VIP treatment: free, primo parking, and access to the loading zone. As SJAA volunteers rolled in with their gear, we loaded onto the scissor lift on the exterior of the ship. This lift brought our gear up to the first level of the Hornet, from which we could waltz right through the museum exhibits on our way to the bomb elevator. As we passed a model of the Apollo 11 command module, the Airstream RV that served as the Astronauts' decontamination chamber, and a full, actual helicopter, we piled our gear to be loaded by Hornet staff. With our gear situated like Tetris blocks, the elevator ushered our instruments up to their final destination: the flight deck!

Once on deck, SJAA volunteers setup at the eastern end of the flight deck, opposite the Robotics competition, and across from the pop-up food tent. Strategically situated between the food and restrooms, we captured the attention of many curious passers-by. We had several stations available for guests to enjoy: white light

solar telescopes and binoculars for sunspots, h-alpha telescopes for prominences, smart scopes streaming live video views of the Sun, a crafts station, and a trivia game! Kids of all ages enjoyed making bracelets out of UV-reactive material, and watching them glow after charging in the sunshine. Many guests came to play our game with hopes of striking it rich in the hourly raffles. We had an astrophysicist, cosplaying storm troopers, and even Snoopy come to enjoy the views through our instruments. Situated right next to the robotics competition, we got many science-minded teenagers and young adults coming by to talk shop and revel in our shared interests.

The whole event was a great success, and the sun seemed to put on a show, just for us. Scores of sunspots dotted the sun in white-light views. Several attendees remarked that they reminded them of an archipelago of islands, like Hawaii or Alaska. Two pairs of white-light binoculars were a popular choice among guests for their wider fields of view, and comfortable viewing angles. The h-alpha scopes revealed many textured edge-on prominences, along with a dramatic, fiery loop protruding from the limb. The weather was perfectly clear all day, allowing for views for the entire seven hours that we were setup.

Our gratitude goes out to Wolf Witt for organizing the SJAA presence and to the USS Hornet staff for hosting us. We are already looking forward to Splashdown 60!

# MEET COSMO

**By Carl Svensson**

*A new AI-powered chat-bot unlocks the secrets of the universe for anyone to understand.*

**THE FIELD OF** astronomy is no stranger to Artificial Intelligence (AI). Professionals and amateurs alike use this tool to do everything from categorizing galaxies, to denoising astrophotographs, or even just asking ChatGPT how to polar align a telescope. Now, there is a new tool out from high school junior, Claire Zhao, and the University of Washington Astronomy Department: Cosmo.

In addition to sharing a name and logo that resemble the telepathic space dog from Guardians of the Galaxy, Cosmo is an AI-powered chatbot specifically trained to answer questions about astronomy for various age groups. Inspired by her own journey to try and learn more about astronomy from a young age, Claire designed Cosmo to be able to explain the often complicated concepts of astronomy in a way that is digestible for children and adults. Cosmo achieves this goal by offering a chatbot experience tailored to one of four age groups: 0-5 years old, 5-10 years old, children over 10, and all ages.

Depending on which mode you select, Cosmo will answer your questions in a different amount of detail, and use simplified language, examples, and tone for younger age ranges. Cosmo maintains a fun and friendly tone throughout each mode, and even throws in a joke related to your query.

To test it out, I asked Cosmo the same question in each of the four modes: What is a star? See the next page in this article for the full conversation. The responses got progressively more detailed, and more scientific as the age range increased. In this experiment, the three youngest age group responses contained cute examples, comparing a star to either a "glowing marshmallow," or a "light bulb." Some of the examples made more sense to me than others, but I'm also nowhere near the target age range for those modes, so maybe it is more relatable for others. For example, my marshmallows do not glow when I cook them over a campfire; they burn to a crisp. But, maybe that's just me.

The response for the oldest age group contained no examples, and stuck more to detailed descriptions about the properties of a star. Cosmo did, however, maintain

an approachable vocabulary, and elaborated on its definitions. Across other experiments, I noticed that Cosmo would take a sidebar to explain concepts like a "light year" as they are introduced in the course of a response to a query. This is a nice touch that seems to appropriately mimic the style of popular science communicators like Neil DeGrasse Tyson and Bill Nye.

As with any Large Language Model (LLM) AI tool, it is important to recognize their limitations. These tools do not actually "know" the answers to your questions, but they are adept at correlating the key words in your question to the data they were trained on, and they are very good at putting together fun and interesting sentences. Most of the time this produces an answer that's correct, but sometimes it only produces an answer that *sounds* correct. These are known as "hallucinations." It is important for any users of this and other AI tools to independently verify any information they produce.

That said, I found the answers to the questions I asked to be consistent with my understanding of the concepts. In fact, the only error I spotted was in a joke offered up at the end of a response to "What is a galaxy?" The joke read: "Why don't aliens visit our galaxy? Because they read the reviews and we only got one star!" Of course, our galaxy has many stars, but this would be a great joke if you replaced "galaxy" with "solar system."

In any case, Cosmo is a fun, and enlightening tool. It really is amazing to see AI technology at work, answering practical and theoretical questions in an instant. It is so much easier to phrase a question exactly as it comes out of my brain, than to try to engineer the correct keywords for a traditional search engine, and then sift through all the results. What might have taken hours to search, sift, read, and synthesize is now done in seconds, and presented in plain English. No need for a mathematics or physics PhD to understand the fascinating concepts of the cosmos.

Cosmo is a great tool to help demystify the secrets of the stars. It seems to carry on the mission statement from its website, which is to "make our universe universal." Speaking of which, you can try it out for yourself at universestudio.org. Give it a whirl!

# What is a star?

## Under 5 Years Old

Hi there, great question! A star is a very big ball of hot, glowing gas in space. Our Sun is a star! Stars can be big or small, dim or bright. They shine because they are very hot, just like a marshmallow gets all glowy when you roast it on a campfire. But remember, stars are much, much hotter than any campfire!

Fun fact: The twinkling stars you see in the sky at night are very far away from us. Some of them are so far that their light takes years to reach Earth! Isn't space awesome?!

And here's a little joke for you: Why did the sun go to school? Because it wanted to get brighter!

## All Ages

Stars are celestial bodies composed mainly of hydrogen and helium that produce their own light and heat through a process called nuclear fusion. To explain this in more detail, let's break it down.

**Composition**: Stars are predominantly composed of hydrogen and helium, with smaller amounts of other elements. These elements are the building blocks of all matter in the universe.

**Nuclear Fusion**: Stars generate their energy through a process called nuclear fusion, where hydrogen atoms combine to form helium. This process releases a tremendous amount of energy in the form of light and heat. The Sun, our nearest star, is a perfect example of a star undergoing nuclear fusion.

**Lifecycle**: Stars go through a lifecycle, starting as a nebula (a cloud of dust and gas in space), then condensing to form a protostar, maturing to become a main sequence star, and finally evolving into a red giant. Depending upon their mass, they eventually end their lives as a supernova, possibly leaving behind a neutron star or black hole.

**Light**: The light we see from stars is due to the energy released by the nuclear fusion process. However, stars are incredibly far away, so their light can take years to reach us – that's why we say the distances in space are measured in 'light-years'.

Now a little star humor for you: Why don't stars like to play hide and seek? Because they'd always be spotted! Feel free to ask more about the vast universe around us!

## 5-10 Years Old

Hello! A star is like a massive, glowing ball of gas in space. You can think of it like a huge, cosmic light bulb! Most of the light we see when we look up at the night sky comes from stars. The most famous star we know is the Sun, which is the star closest to us.

Stars are born in places called nebulae, which are like big, fluffy space clouds full of gas and dust. Over time, gravity pulls this gas and dust together to form a star.

Once a star is formed, it burns for millions or even billions of years, thanks to a process called nuclear fusion. This is where hydrogen atoms in the star's core smash together to create helium, releasing a lot of energy in the form of light and heat. And that's how stars shine!

But remember, stars aren't actually star-shaped. They just look pointy from Earth because they are so far away and their light gets a bit squiggly as it travels through our atmosphere.

Now, here's a fun fact: If stars went to school, they would be the "brightest" students in class! Keep exploring, space detective!

## 10+ Years Old

Great question! Simply put, a star is a huge, shining ball of hot gas that produces light and heat through a process called nuclear fusion. This happens in the star's core or center.

Here's how it works: Stars are mostly made of hydrogen and during nuclear fusion, hydrogen atoms smash together to form helium. This process releases a lot of energy in the form of light and heat, which makes the stars shine bright! The Sun, which is the closest star to us, appears bigger and brighter than other stars because it's much closer to us.

Fun fact, if you were to compare a star to a fruit, it would probably be a watermelon! Not because of the size, oh no no, stars are millions of times bigger than Earth! It's more about the structure. The core of the star would be the juicy fruit part of the watermelon, where all the nuclear fusion happens. The rind or the green part of the watermelon would be like the surface of the star that we can see.

Remember, just like you wouldn't be able to eat a star (too hot!), you shouldn't look directly at them, especially the Sun, without proper safety equipment. It could harm your eyes!

My interest in Astronomy stems from observing the night sky. # Stellar Aberration
**by Jordan Makower**

**STELLAR ABERRATION** is a term I came across many years ago, when I started studying astronomy. I didn't understand it then. What was it? Could I observe it? How would it affect my observations? Modern electronic telescopes give us the opportunity to observe, record, and use this phenomenon.

"SA" is the apparent displacement of a star (or stars) off their charted positions, due to the Earth's orbit around the Sun. It affects *all* stars, so if you are doing precision work (looking for a nova, galaxy, binary star, etc, and following it) over time, you'd want to know where to look. James Bradley, working on a different problem, in the late 1720's, uncovered observational data that didn't fit in with what he was looking for. He was trying to measure the distances to select stars by their parallax shift during the year. The numbers he found were too large to be accounted for by parallax, and they were similar periodic numbers for every star!

He was using a long, highly accurate, transit telescope, with a micrometer reticle eyepiece. This type of device is employed to examine stars which cross an imaginary line, directly overhead, the observer's zenith. The graph below, from Wikipedia, shows his data. Two stars shown are Gamma Draconis and 35 Camelopardalis, ones that passed almost directly over his location in Wanstead, North-East London. The graph shows each star's displacement off its declination position, as measured in seconds of angular arc. Do you notice something that could cause this? (Wanstead is located at 51° 34' 43.5288"

N, Gamma Draconis is at 51° 29' 20.0242" Declination, 35 Camelopardalis is at 51° 34' 24.6" Declination)

I have a Stellina (Vaonis) scope. To observe this displacement, I would have to use its field of view (1° x 0.7°) to do my calculations. The object that I would be looking for could be 'off center' (electronically fixed position) by as much as 20 angular seconds. Since these scopes capture the light of so many stars, the one I would want to observe might not be in the center of my scope's field of view!

Have you figured out that the shift being plotted is caused by the change in the Earth's direction during the year? Like driving through a rainstorm, the apparent direction of raindrops falling is caused by the car's motion. This is like the apparent change in position of a star due to the Earth's orbital motion.
